# Tense System

Originally, the verbal system of languages was based on the opposition between **perfective** and **imperfective** actions. In other words, it was not originally a tense system at all. A genuine tense system consisting of three members - **past**, **present**, and **future** - emerged only later.

Perfective and imperfective forms are distinguished either by means of inflectional endings or through vowel/consonant alternation within the root. In some languages, both mechanisms are used simultaneously.

Examples:

- **German:** `tue – tat`
- **English:** `do – did`
- **Spanish:** `hago – hice`
- **Italian:** `faccio – feci`
- **Latin:** `scribo – scripsi`
- **Old Russian:** `творю – творих`

In many modern languages, these historical patterns are difficult to observe. In English, for example, many inflectional endings disappeared over time, while in Russian an entire past tense paradigm was lost. Therefore, earlier stages of these languages must be examined in order to understand their historical development. Another example is Modern Turkish.

- **Turkish:** `ederim – ettim`

In Modern Turkish there are no verbal forms created simply by combining a root with an ending. The present and past forms contain the suffixes **-er** and **-t**, respectively. However, Old Turkic possessed a present-tense form such as **sebür**, consisting of the root **seb** and the ending **-ür**.

I am also inclined to believe that, in the Germanic languages, all verbs were originally strong verbs. Over time, however, and under the influence of other languages, only the most frequently used verbs retained their strong conjugation.

For example, the English verb **help – helped – helped** was formerly conjugated as:

`helpan – healp – holpen`

This understanding of tense and its historical development allows us to make the following observations:

- Originally, the present and future were not distinguished grammatically and were expressed only lexically. This explains why present-tense verb forms can often refer to future events.
- Future-tense forms developed later and were frequently created by analytical means.
- It also explains why the subjunctive I and subjunctive II exist despite having only binary opposition: **would do** versus **would have done**.

# Prepositions and Cases

Prepositions can be divided into three groups according to their origin.

## Simple Prepositions

Simple prepositions are underived forms that have existed in the language from its earliest stages. They are generally short and have never functioned independently as adverbs. This is questionable, however, as it is very difficult to trace back to the earliest stages. For now, though, this is what I believe to be true.

## Adverbial Prepositions

These are derived prepositions that developed either from adverbs or from combinations of a preposition and an adverb.

## Nominal Prepositions

These are derived prepositions that originated from nominal parts of speech (such as nouns or verbs) or from combinations of a preposition with a nominal element.

### Examples in English

**Simple**

- in
- on
- to

**Adverbial**

- up
- out
- down
- above

**Nominal**

- between
- during

### Examples in Russian

It is an interesting example of an absence of one group, since most Russian adverbs originate from nominal parts of speech.

**Simple**

- в
- на
- до

**Adverbial**

- (none)

**Nominal**

- для
- мимо
- возле

While nominal prepositions retain a clearly identifiable nominal element, the distinction between simple and adverbial prepositions often becomes blurred over time. Consequently, prepositions such as **for** (English), **vor** (German), and **pro** (Latin) are no longer perceived as originating from adverbs.

Simple prepositions are closely connected with grammatical cases. Cases are therefore capable of expressing all the functions performed by simple prepositions.

This can be observed in Turkish, where simple prepositions are essentially absent. Instead, the language employs the following cases:

- **Locative** — *where?*  
  Indicates the location of something. It is used both for place and for time.

- **Dative** — *to whom? to what?*  
  Indicates movement toward a destination. It also expresses purpose.

- **Ablative** — *from whom? from what?*  
  Indicates movement away from a source. It can also express cause.

In languages that do possess simple prepositions, these perform the same semantic functions:

- **Location**
- **Direction toward an endpoint**
- **Direction away from a starting point**

It is important to note that when a language has grammatical cases, one particular case often predominates within each semantic group of prepositions. This tendency can be observed clearly in the following languages.

### Russian

#### Location

- в (Locative / Prepositional)
- под (Instrumental)
- над (Instrumental)
- на (Prepositional)

#### Direction Toward an Endpoint

- в (Accusative)
- под (Accusative)
- по (Accusative)
- на (Accusative)

#### Direction Away from a Starting Point

- из (Genitive)
- от (Genitive)
- с (Genitive)

### German

#### Location

- in (Dative)
- an (Dative)
- auf (Dative)
- vor (Dative)
- bei* (Dative)
- um* (Accusative)

#### Direction Toward an Endpoint

- in (Accusative)
- an (Accusative)
- bis* (Accusative)
- zu* (Dative)
- auf (Accusative)
- vor (Accusative)

#### Direction Away from a Starting Point

- ab (Dative)
- aus (Dative)
- von (Dative)

In German, most prepositions govern the **dative case** when they denote location and the **accusative case** when they denote direction. There are, however, exceptions (*), which always govern a particular case regardless of meaning.

## Presence

There is another semantic group of prepositions: those expressing **presence** or **absence**. Thus, there are four groups in total.

This group always consists of two opposing prepositions. In many languages, one of them is a simple preposition, while the other is a derived preposition.

Examples:

**English**
- with
- without

**German**
- mit
- ohne

# Sentence Members

There are only four **content-bearing sentence members**.

1. **Subject** — a principal sentence member. The subject answers the questions associated with the nominative case ("who?" or "what?").

2. **Predicate** — a principal sentence member. The predicate does not answer a question; instead, it conveys new information about the subject.

3. **Object** — a principal sentence member when the verb is transitive. The object answers the questions associated with the accusative, dative, and genitive cases.

4. **Modifier (Attribute)** — a secondary sentence member. A modifier can answer a wide variety of questions, depending on the word it modifies.

## Subject and Predicate

The **subject** and **predicate** are obligatory sentence members.

A sentence without a predicate cannot exist because it communicates no information. Likewise, a sentence without a subject cannot exist because there is nothing with which the predicate can agree in person and number. If either appears to be missing, it is either omitted or understood from the context.

Examples in Russian:

- Дождь (идёт). — "It is raining." (lit. "Rain (is falling).")
- (оно) Смеркалось. — "It was getting dark." (lit. "(It) was getting dark.")

Predicates occur in only two forms:

- **Verbal predicate** — always simple.
- **Nominal predicate** — always compound.

A nominal predicate may itself contain a transitive relationship, for example:

- *to make the game interesting*
- *made him come*

## Object

An object is an obligatory sentence member when the verb is **transitive**, but mostly a secondary member when the verb is **intransitive**, in which case it functions as an indirect object.

A verb can have only **one direct object**.

Indirect objects fall into two semantic categories:

- direction **toward** an object;
- direction **away from** an object.

Not every language distinguishes these two categories.

Examples in Russian:

- написать **другу** (dative case) — "to write **to a friend**" — recipient
- бояться **пауков** (genitive case) — "to fear **spiders**" — source of fear

The everyday English expression *to be afraid of spiders* is very similar to the Russian expression. *of* likewise indicates the **source of fear**, corresponding semantically to the Russian genitive.

A **direct object** cannot be expressed with a preposition.

From a purely syntactic point of view, all prepositional indirect objects function as modifiers rather than objects. Nevertheless, the distinction between the following constructions is largely conventional:

- *He sent me a letter.* (indirect object)
- *He sent a letter to me.* (modifer)

## Modifier (Attribute)

A modifier exists only as part of a subject, predicate, or object. It may modify any lexical part of speech.

Examples:

- *(large) park*
- *run (quickly)*
- *(most) accurate*
- *(very) hot*

Modifiers may themselves be expressed by different parts of speech, including adjectives, adverbs, and infinitives.

Examples:

- *asked (someone) to come*
